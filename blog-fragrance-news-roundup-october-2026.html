Fragrance News Roundup: October 2026

Fragrance News  ·  October 19, 2026  ·  5 min read

Three stories from this month say a lot about where fragrance is heading right now. A major conglomerate is trying to get closer to the people actually driving fragrance trends online. Perfumers are rethinking what a gourmand fragrance is allowed to smell like. And more people are treating scent the way they treat an outfit, something built in layers rather than picked once and worn the same way every day. Here is what is actually happening, as of October 19, 2026, with the sources behind each story at the bottom.

Coty wants a seat at the table with fragrance creators

On October 15, 2026, Coty announced the launch of its Global Fragrance Club in Paris. It is not a product. It is a standing community that brings together fragrance experts, educators, content creators, and community leaders from Europe, the UK, the US, and China, and connects them directly with Coty's own perfumers, including Nathalie Lorson, Amandine Clerc-Marie, and Fabrice Pellegrin. The plan is an ongoing exchange rather than a one-off launch event: workshops, discussions, and co-creation that continue after the Paris kickoff.

Why bother setting this up at all? Coty's own announcement points to a shift that most fragrance shoppers already feel: social platforms have handed a lot of influence over what people buy to voices that are not fragrance houses at all, and TikTok is now cited as the single biggest influence on fragrance purchases among Gen Z shoppers. Coty framed the club as a recognition that, after more than a century in the business, the culture surrounding fragrance keeps moving faster than any one company can track alone, a remark credited to Jean Holtzmann, Chief Brands Officer at Coty Prestige. In plain terms, the people reviewing bottles on TikTok now carry real weight over what a company like Coty decides to make next, and Coty would rather sit in the room with them than catch up to a trend after it has already peaked.

Gourmands are growing up

For a while, "gourmand" mostly meant something broadly sweet, vanilla, caramel, sugar, dessert in a bottle. Coverage of fall 2026 trends suggests that is getting more specific. Perfumers are now pairing sweet notes with coffee, salt, smoke, tea, and savory spice instead of leaning on sugar by itself, and trend data cited from Spate names pistachio, caramel, honey, and matcha among the fastest-growing notes people are searching for right now.

A few fragrances named in that coverage show the shift. DedCool's Mocha Magic pairs espresso with tonka bean, cream, and sandalwood. ROJA London's Espresso Aoud puts roasted coffee and caramelized pistachio against oud. A tea-focused launch from STORIES Parfums leans into the same idea from a different angle. None of these three are in the ParfAI catalog yet, so there is nothing to link to for the fragrances themselves, but the notes behind them already are.

Jonny Webber of PerfumeDirect.com summed up the shift this way: gourmands "aren't disappearing, they are simply growing up," because, as he put it, shoppers are looking for something that does the unexpected. If you want to see which fragrances already in our own catalog play with coffee, tonka bean, or a sweeter edge, those note pages are a good place to start.

Perfume is being worn in layers now, not picked once

The third story is less about one company and more about how people are actually wearing fragrance day to day. Coverage citing Euromonitor International's 2026 Voice of the Consumer survey reports that a third of Gen Z shoppers globally now say unique combinations of fragrance notes are something they actively look for, and that shoppers are increasingly building what one analyst called a fragrance wardrobe, several scents mixed and matched instead of one signature scent worn the same way every day.

People quoted on the trend describe a fairly simple method: pick one shared note, something like sandalwood, vanilla, musk, or jasmine, and use it as a bridge between two fragrances worn together, on skin and on clothing, or split between different pulse points. It is close to what our own Layering Recipe Builder already does, pairing fragrances that share a note so the combination makes sense instead of just clashing on your skin.

What this means if you're just trying to find your next scent

None of this changes what actually smells good on you, but it is a reasonable explanation for why new releases keep reaching for stranger note combinations, and why layering sets keep showing up in more fragrance houses' lineups.
